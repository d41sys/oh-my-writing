# Paragraphs and sentences

Load when: drafting the blocks of a piece (stage 9), or when a draft reads as a list of points, or when readers lose the thread between sentences. These are defaults for English prose; the exceptions say when to break them.

## The paragraph's job

- A paragraph does one job: lands one point, tells one beat, gives one example, or handles one objection. Two points need two paragraphs.
- Open with the point; put the evidence in the middle; close by tightening the point or handing off to the next. A key sentence buried in the middle is read by fewer people.
- Paragraph breaks follow the argument, not a length quota. A run of paragraphs of identical size is a signal to check whether the breaks follow the thought, not to chop or pad.
- Treadmill test: for each paragraph ask what is new here. If nothing, cut it or merge into the paragraph that carries the point.
- Exception: narrative and some essays hold the point back for a turn; there the paragraph's job is to set up the turn and the point arrives in its last lines. State that as the job.

## Old before new

- Start a sentence from something the reader already has (the previous sentence's ending, a grounded concept, the subject of the piece), then add the new information.
- At section level: recall what the reader has before asking them to take in something new. A section that opens with a recap of the previous one is a different failure; link by the question the last section left open, not by a summary.
- Before any equation, table, figure, code listing, or result, say what it is for. The reader should know what to look for before they look.
- Before the general rule, give the concrete case when the reader is meeting the idea for the first time; the reader who knows it can skim.

## Topic and stress positions

- The start of a sentence is the topic position: the old information and the thing the sentence is about.
- The end is the stress position: the new or important information lands there.
- Compare: "Accuracy rises by 15% when attention is used" buries the cause; "When attention is used, accuracy rises by 15%" puts the result at the stress position. Which is better depends on whether the cause or the result is new.
- Keep the subject and verb close; a long insertion between them makes the reader hold the subject in memory.
- Do not end a sentence on a weak trailing phrase ("as well", "in some cases", "to a certain extent") unless it carries a real condition.

## Verbs and actors

- Put actions in verbs and actors in subjects: "the team analysed the logs", not "an analysis of the logs was performed"; "the job fails when disk fills", not "failure of the job occurs upon disk exhaustion".
- Watch nouns made from verbs and adjectives (-tion, -ment, -ity, -ance): they hide the actor and slow the sentence. Convert when it clarifies.
- Active voice by default. Passive is fine where the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or the object is the topic; never use it to hide who made a choice.
- Prefer plain "is/has" to "serves as/stands as/boasts" when the sentence just states a fact.
- Replace vague words with what they stand for: "performance" -> the measure; "large" -> the number; "improves" -> from what to what. Use only numbers present in the material. If the material gives none, write the sentence without a figure (or say that the material gives none) and ask the author in the reply.
- Exception: a passive that keeps the topic stable across sentences can read better than switching subjects.

## One term per concept

- Choose one name for each concept and use it everywhere: text, headings, figure labels, table headers. Changing the word for variety reads as a change of concept.
- Introduce a named concept where you name it: land the idea and its name together, once. Later uses rely on that.
- If the author's term is jargon the reader lacks, define it at first use or replace it with the plain description; do not change it silently halfway through.
- Replace a bare "this", "it", "they", "that" with a noun when the reference could be missed ("This ablation shows..."). A pronoun pointing at an entire previous paragraph usually needs a noun.
- Do not coin a new label when an existing word fits; a coinage costs definition space.
- Exception: quoted speech and fiction may vary terms because people do.

## Connective logic

Connectives make a relation visible; they never create it. If the relation is not in the material, the connective is a lie.

| Relation | Connectives | Misuse |
|---|---|---|
| Cause or result | because, so, therefore, as a result | "Therefore" after a non-sequitur |
| Contrast | but, however, although, yet | "However" with no actual opposition |
| Addition | also, and, likewise | chains of "moreover / furthermore" used as filler |
| Example | for example, such as | an example that illustrates nothing in the sentence before |
| Order | first, then, finally | numbering items that have no order |
| Summary | in short, so | a last sentence that repeats the paragraph |

- If two paragraphs could swap places and nobody would notice, there is no through-line. Find the question the first raises that the second answers; if none exists, report the gap rather than adding a connective.
- Staged run-ups before a point ("here is the thing", "it is worth noting that") and frames that announce importance ("here is what stood out") add nothing; state the point.
- Say why something matters only when the material gives the reason; do not invent stakes.

## Rhythm and length

- One idea per sentence is a default, not a law. Split sentences much over 35-40 words when the reader must hold too much; leave long sentences that move cleanly.
- Vary sentence length by clarifying ideas, not by chopping sentences into fragments or adding quotas. Uniform rhythm is worth a second look only when it also lacks substance.
- Avoid consecutive sentences with the same opener unless the repetition is deliberate.
- Keep real lists of three as three; do not add or drop an item to fit a pattern.
- Cut sentences that restate an earlier sentence, filler intensifiers, and signposts that narrate the layout ("In this section we will...") unless the sentence tells the reader how evidence is organised in a way they could not see.
- Precision beats brevity when they conflict. Keep claim-defining qualifiers attached to their claims.

## Hand-offs between blocks

- Where the next block does not follow on its own, end a block with what it picks up: the question left open, the term about to be needed, or the contrast about to be drawn. Say it plainly, in the author's voice; do not announce the structure ("The next section", "Having seen this"). Where the next block follows naturally, just stop.
- Begin a block with its message. The reader should know where they are from what it says, not from a label or a restated outline line.
- Add a bridge sentence only when the relation exists in the material. A bridge that invents a relation turns an outline into a story the author did not tell.
- Headings name what the section contains or says; decorative headings, emoji prefixes and catchy labels give the skim reader nothing.

## Other languages

Structure, grounding, one-term-per-concept, and the paragraph job transfer to any language. The English-specific advice about positions at the start and end of a sentence, zombie nouns, and connective choice does not transfer automatically: sentence order, topic marking and connective habits differ by language. When drafting in Vietnamese or another language, apply the structural rules from SKILL.md and let the author's own samples, or the language's own norms, decide the sentence-level choices. Say so in the note when lexical or positional checks were skipped.
