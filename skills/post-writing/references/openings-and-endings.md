Load when: writing or repairing a title, headline, hook, opening paragraph or ending; deciding whether context may precede the artifact; replacing stock closers.

# Openings, titles and endings

## What an opening has to do

In one to three lines the reader decides whether the piece is for them. An opening works when it shows the subject, not when it announces one. The concrete thing can be an artifact (a command, a log line, a chart), a number with its condition, a scene, a sharp claim the piece will defend, or a question the reader already has.

Choose the opening from the author's real material. If the best artifact is missing, open on the strongest true claim the material supports and ask for the artifact in the reply: `[ASK AUTHOR: a real number, incident or example to open on]`. Do not write a vivid but invented scene, and do not leave a blank in the opening.

## Do cold readers need context first?

Throat-clearing and necessary context look alike. Test:

1. Remove the first sentence. Does the next sentence still make sense to the target reader?
2. If yes, the first sentence was warm-up. Cut it.
3. If no, keep only the minimum premise and put the artifact right after it.

Examples of warm-up that fails the test: era statements ("in today's fast-changing world"), importance statements ("X is more important than ever"), definitions the reader already holds, thanks and apologies for the post itself, and mood announcements on launches ("We're thrilled to announce", "Excited to share", "Big news") that name a feeling instead of the change. Context that passes: "This is about our job queue, which retries failed jobs three times" before a log excerpt a newcomer could not decode.

## Opening moves

Rotate moves. A feed or a newsletter archive that uses one move every time becomes predictable. Bracketed blanks in the examples below are for you: fill them from the author's facts, or pick another move. A bracket never reaches the published post, and the examples are not content to reuse.

| Move | Use when | Needs from the author | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Artifact first | The piece is technical or practical | A real snippet, output, screenshot or figure | The artifact is unreadable without context; add one line |
| Number with condition | There is a measured result | The figure, the baseline and how it was measured | A bare number with no baseline; an inflated round-up |
| Scene | The piece is a story or a post-mortem | The real moment, with its details | Invented detail; scene too long before the point |
| Sharp claim | The piece argues | A claim the body will defend | A claim the body softens or abandons |
| Reader's question | The piece answers a known question | The question as readers actually phrase it | A question the piece does not answer |
| Confession or correction | The author was wrong and learned | What the author believed, what changed their mind | Performed humility; no concrete content |
| Before and after | The change is visible | Both states, honestly shown | A flattering "before" that was never real |
| Proof first | Credibility is the obstacle | A receipt: result, demo or link | Proof that does not support the claim |

- **Pick one example, not three.** Chaining several parallel examples in the opener dilutes each. Choose the most vivid real one and put the rest in the body.
- **A curiosity gap must close.** Withholding the key noun is fair if the piece supplies it soon. A gap that never closes is clickbait and costs the reader's trust.
- **A bold claim must be specific enough to be wrong.** "Most cost spikes come from idle compute, not traffic" can be tested; "efficiency matters" cannot.
- **Contrarian only if true.** Do not manufacture a counter-intuitive angle to stop the scroll. If the real finding is ordinary, open on the real artifact.
- **Lived-in detail comes from the author.** A confession, scene or point-of-view opening works only with the specific detail the author has (the time, the number, what was said). A generic version is invisible and an invented one breaks trust; if the detail is missing, choose a different move and ask in the reply (`[ASK AUTHOR: ...]`).

## Titles and headlines

A title is a promise. The test: after reading, would the reader feel the title kept its promise?

1. State the subject and the payoff with the words a reader would search or use. "How I reduced build time from `[A]` to `[B]`" promises a number and a method; the piece must give both.
2. Prefer the specific over the clever. A clever title is allowed if a subtitle or the first line makes the subject plain.
3. Numbers in titles must match the content. A list with a count in the title has exactly that many substantive items.
4. Avoid claims the piece cannot support: "the only", "everything you need", "never again", "secret".
5. Use a question title only when the piece answers it in the first screen.
6. For long web posts, put the primary subject terms in the title and in the first heading naturally. For a title shown in search results, a length near the display limit is a heuristic to verify; do not shorten at the cost of meaning.
7. Offer two or three title options when the angle is open, and state in one phrase what each promises.

Section headings state the point of the section ("Retries hid the real failure") rather than a category ("Results"), unless a venue's template requires the category.

## Hooks for short channels

- The first line must work alone, because the channel truncates.
- Name the topic in concrete nouns within the first few words.
- Hook phrase shapes that work when the blanks are true: "`[Result]` after changing `[specific thing]`", "I was wrong about `[belief]`: `[what the evidence showed]`", "`[Common advice]` cost us `[specific result]`". Without the real result, do not use them.
- When writing hooks for video, plan the visual action, the spoken line and the on-screen text as three separate tracks. The text must not just repeat the voice. The next few seconds must continue the hook's premise rather than switch to a pitch.
- If a hook gets attention but the reader leaves at the first paragraph, the promise and the content do not match; fix the content or the hook.

## Endings

An ending is the last thing the reader carries away. Choose by the type of piece:

| Piece | Good ending | Weak ending |
|---|---|---|
| How-to | The expected result and one next step, or the most common failure | A restatement of the steps |
| Argument | The consequence for the reader, or the single condition that would change the author's mind | "In conclusion..." restating reasons |
| Story or reflection | An earned insight or a real open question; the ending can open a door | A moral the events do not carry |
| Post-mortem | What changed in practice and what is still unknown | A generic list of lessons |
| Announcement | Where to try it and where to read more | Thanks and hype |
| Newsletter | One next action or a short sign-off that sets up the next issue | A pile of "see you next week" lines |
| Social post | A specific, answerable question the author wants replies to, or a link with no ornament | A stock prompt |

- **Stop on content.** If the last paragraph only restates the previous ones, delete it. If the last sentence explains why the previous sentence matters and the reader can already see why, delete that too.
- **No aphorism close.** A short quotable line that claims significance ("That is the real lesson.") works only when the body has earned it; usually it signals that the piece lacks a stronger last point.
- **A real next action** names the thing to do, where, and ideally the expected result: "Run `[command]` and compare the output against the table above."
- **A question** is fine when the author wants replies and it is specific ("How do you handle `[situation]` in your setup?"). Do not add one to every piece.
- **Stock closers to rewrite, not just delete:** "Bookmark this", "Thank me later", "Let that sink in", "Thoughts?", "Agree?", "Don't sleep on this", "You won't want to miss this", "This one is worth your time". They claim value without adding it. Replace each with the specific reason to read or act, or with nothing.
- **Do not invent a call to action, closing question or moral the author did not ask for.**
- **Promotional endings** are fine on a promotional piece when the next step is real and clear. Do not attach a sales call to a reflective or informational post.

## Quick repair for a weak opening or ending

1. Mark the first and last sentence.
2. Ask of each: would the reader miss this if it were gone?
3. If not, delete it and read the new first or last sentence again.
4. If the piece now starts or stops too abruptly for the channel, add back only the minimum premise or the next action, not a flourish.
5. Check the title against the final text one more time.
