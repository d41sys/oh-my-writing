Load when: choosing the section order, density, voice, or typical traps for a specific report type.

# Report types

Each type below gives the reader's job, a default spine, the voice and density to aim for, and the usual failure. Spines are defaults. Drop a section the reader will not use; never drop the evidence trail. Lengths are habits observed in practice, not quotas: a short report that decides is better than a long one that surveys.

## How to pick

| The reader needs to | Type |
|---|---|
| Choose between options or approve a course | Decision memo |
| Understand a topic well enough to act or to ask better questions | Research report |
| Understand a market, a segment, or rivals | Market or competitive report |
| Grasp a long report in a few minutes | Executive summary |
| Know whether work is on track and what needs them | Status report |
| Trust figures and a story before putting money in | Investor materials |
| Learn what went wrong and prevent a repeat | Post-mortem |
| Check something against a standard and see what fails | Audit or diagnostic report |
| Keep a reusable lesson from hard or surprising work | Lessons-learned entry |

When a request fits two types (a research report that ends in a decision), write the decision memo on top and attach the research as the evidence section or appendix.

## Voice and density by type

Voice is a dial set by the genre and the reader. Warm does not mean exclamatory and cool does not mean flat. Mismatch is a defect: a status update written like a pitch, or a post-mortem written like a press release.

| Type | Register | Density | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decision memo | Cool, direct, active | High | Recommendation in the first lines (when the notes leave the choice open, that it is open and where the evidence points); opinionated but fair to the options it rejected |
| Research report | Cool, data-led | High | Findings before background; every number with a baseline |
| Competitive report | Cool, fair | High | Honest about the client's weak spots; no brochure language about rivals |
| Executive summary | Plain, compact | Highest | Standalone; no method; no new claims |
| Status report | Terse, factual | Low prose, high signal | Change since last report first |
| Investor materials | Plain, specific, slightly warmer | Moderate | Verifiable claims; no puffery; assumptions visible |
| Post-mortem | Plain, calm, specific | Moderate | Blameless; concrete timeline; no drama |

An objective register fails in two ways: exaggeration, and flat accuracy that gives no reason to care. After each section ask whether a reader who does not already care would keep going. If not, state the consequence for the reader or the mechanism that makes the difference, never a claim that cannot be verified.

Concessions ("the data does not cover X") run at full strength in research and decision writing; they build trust. In investor material state limits too, but in plain sentences, not apologies.

## Decision memo

**Reader's job:** approve, choose, or redirect.

**Spine:**

1. Recommendation (one to three sentences; when the notes leave the choice open, that it is open and where the evidence points), confidence and its reason, decision needed and by when.
2. Why now and what is at stake (short; the reader usually knows the context).
3. Options considered, each with what it costs and gives up. Include doing nothing or the current arrangement only when the notes describe it.
4. Evidence behind the recommendation, ordered by what the reader will challenge first.
5. Risks: for each, the effect on the recommendation. Name a threshold or tripwire only when the notes or author give one; do not add a trial, gate or deadline to a recommendation the author stated, and say the risk the author accepts.
6. Next steps with owners and dates, if approved.
7. Appendix: method, data, source ledger, rejected options in more detail.

**Density:** typically one to two pages of body; the appendix may be long.

**Short memo (under about 600 words):** one opening paragraph holds the recommendation, the main reason, and the one doubt that matters and why, in plain sentences; the options and risks follow as short paragraphs under at most two or three plain headings. No separate summary, no labelled "Confidence:" or "Decision requested:" lines, no announced "my confidence is moderate", and no caveat that appears both in the opening and again below.

**Decision record variant:** for a standing technical or organizational decision, keep the same content as a compact record: status, context, options considered, decision, reasoning, consequences accepted. Keep it findable and short; link the evidence rather than paste it.

**Traps:** a recommendation buried after the background; options listed without what each costs; a "balanced" memo that never takes a position; a risk section that lists risks without the effect on the recommendation; weights hidden in a score.

## Research or analysis report

**Reader's job:** understand, then act or direct further work.

**Spine:**

1. Executive summary (see below).
2. Question and scope: the decision or question this serves, and what is out of scope.
3. Method and limits: sources, period, definitions, data you did not have. Short in the body, detailed in the appendix.
4. Findings: usually three to five, each as a claim, its evidence with baseline, and a "so what" for this reader. Organize by the question being answered, not by the order discovered.
5. Comparison or analysis section, if the report compares things (load the comparisons reference).
6. Implications and recommendations.
7. Confidence and gaps: where sources conflict, where coverage is thin, what would resolve it.
8. Appendix: full source list with dates, raw data, calculations, glossary if needed.

**Finding first, not pain first.** Open with the discovery, not with a tour of the problem. Background answers one question: why should this reader care now?

**Finding quality test.** Strip a finding of the research behind it. If it still stands, the report did not need the research for it. If the opposite of a finding is absurd, it is a platitude. Findings that survive both are the report.

**Depth through perspectives.** For an unfamiliar or many-sided topic, look at it from several stakeholder angles (for instance performance, reliability and security, operators, cost and governance), but pick the angles the topic needs, not a fixed number. Include one generalist angle that covers the basic facts so fundamentals are not crowded out. When angles disagree, the synthesis should integrate them rather than list what each said. Decompose the question into three to five concrete sub-questions before searching and structure the findings around them.

**Density:** habit is roughly 1,500 to 6,000 words depending on whether it is a brief, competitive report, or white paper. Length follows the number of findings that matter, not the other way round.

**Traps:** a catalog of facts with no thesis; findings without "so what"; background longer than findings; predictions with no basis; numbers without comparison; a method section that hides what data was missing.

## Market report

**Reader's job:** size or characterize a market and know how far to trust the numbers.

Everything in the research spine applies, plus these specifics.

- **Fix the measurement contract first.** What counts and what does not, who buys and pays, geography, period or as-of date, measure (stock or flow, gross or net), currency and base year (nominal or real), classification version, denominator. Estimates that do not share a contract are not comparable; say so when you put them side by side.
- **Size from two directions.** Top-down from a published aggregate and bottom-up from customers times addressable fraction times quantity times price, and reconcile the gap. Write down each logic leap. Treat the total addressable, serviceable, and obtainable figures as scenarios under stated assumptions, not facts about the world and never a guaranteed share.
- **Guard against double counting.** Typical sources: manufacturer plus distributor revenue; parent plus subsidiary; bundle plus components; installed base plus new transactions; overlapping filters applied one after another. Use mutually exclusive segments, one denominator, and show an unallocated residual rather than forcing the total.
- **Label every figure** as reported fact, estimate, calculation, forecast, opinion, or recommendation, and keep the ledger described in [comparisons and recommendations](comparisons-and-recommendations.md).
- **A figure is not true because a paid report prints it.** Cite the original producer where possible, note when several outlets repeat one origin, and do not count mirrors as independent confirmation.
- **Growth rates:** show start value, end value, and number of periods for any compound rate; it hides volatility and breaks and is undefined from a zero or negative start. Do not mix nominal and real values in one total.
- **Stocks and flows:** an installed base, headcount, or capacity is a stock; revenue and shipments are flows. Converting one to the other needs a stated turnover, utilization, or replacement-cycle assumption.
- **Forecasts:** build paths from named drivers, separate history from forecast visually, give a downside and an upside, and do not attach probabilities to scenarios without a model that produces them.

**Traps:** one big number with no definition; a market "size" that is a vendor's headline; CAGR with no endpoints; confident forecasts from a single source.

## Competitive report

**Reader's job:** decide who to treat as the real rivals, how to differentiate, and where to stand.

The report answers three questions, and every section earns its place by moving toward them: who do we compete with, how do we compete, and where is the position we can defend.

**Before assembling:** lock the brief. What is the client's differentiator, who do they sell to, which two tensions define their target position (for example memorability versus credibility, or developer ergonomics versus strict enterprise controls), and what must not be traded away. Scoring against no brief drifts. Finish each competitor profile before assembling; partial profiles undermine every map built on them.

**Spine:**

1. Executive summary: three to five takeaways. Where the client is strong, where exposed, who sits in the target position, top two or three moves. No method here.
2. Landscape and category framing: define the category, then a map on two axes that matter, placing every profiled competitor and the client so crowding is visible. Follow it with one or two sentences interpreting it.
3. Tiers: direct, adjacent, aspirational. A rival is direct only when close on positioning, specialization, size, and market together. One short paragraph per tier.
4. Benchmark matrix: competitors by criteria, grouped by tier, with an honest row for the client. Show both poles of any tension as separate columns. No blended total column. Call out where the client leads and trails.
5. Deep dives on three to five rivals chosen for what they teach, not for rank: the best example of the target position, a cautionary case that holds only one pole, the competent but forgettable archetype, and any direct threat. For each: what they do, what to learn, what to avoid.
6. White space and threats: argue the ownable position from the map and matrix, and check that it is really open. Threats include rivals closing gaps, substitutes (in-house, generalists, automated tools), category shifts, and the client's own exposure.
7. Recommendations, sequenced by impact against effort, each audited against the brief: does copying this practice erode what the client is?
8. Appendix: criteria, weights and rubric anchors, the scoped set and tiers, per-competitor sources, which claims are proven and which only asserted.

**Close with decisions, not admiration.** Questions that help a team align: Is the target position still open or is a rival moving in? Which direct rival is the sharpest threat in the next planning horizon, and what is the counter? Does the identity balance still hold? Which trailing criterion is worth closing and which to concede on purpose? What single move widens distinctiveness most without costing credibility?

**Habits:** use a decision-relevant shortlist instead of a census, and justify inclusions and exclusions; a skewed shortlist can make the client look unbeatable or doomed. Look past the well-marketed names for strong low-profile players. When arguing a rival's limits, also inspect what they advertise as their strength; the strength often hides the deeper limit. Typical length is roughly 1,500 to 3,000 words plus appendix.

**Table stakes versus differentiators:** list each claimed feature and ask whether every comparable rival also has it. If so it is table stakes and is mentioned in passing, not listed as a strength. Each remaining differentiator says how this one differs, not "we also have it", and a differentiator for the client needs customer, win and loss, or review evidence, otherwise it is a hypothesis.

**Traps:** methodology first; scores without the tension map; a matrix with a blended total; deep dives chosen by ranking; recommendations that ignore the brief; brochure language adopted as fact.

## Executive summary

**Reader's job:** know what the report found and what to do without reading it.

**Standalone test:** give the summary, and nothing else, to someone who has not seen the report. They must be able to state the finding, the number that matters with its baseline, the recommendation, how sure the author is, and what is being asked of them.

**Shape:**

1. The finding or recommendation in one to three sentences. When the notes leave the choice open, say so here and give the evidence's direction instead of a recommendation.
2. Three to five supporting points, each with its key number and baseline.
3. Confidence and the main caveat.
4. The decision or next step, with date.

**Rules of thumb:** habit is about 300 words or one page, whichever is smaller. Write it last, after the body is stable. Every figure in it appears in the body with a source. Do not introduce a metric, term, or claim that the body and appendix do not contain. Put no method or background here.

**Revise or summarize mode:** read the whole report first, then write; preserve the report's qualifiers and confidence level. If the report's conclusion is unclear, say so to the author instead of inventing one.

**Traps:** an abstract that describes the report ("this report examines") rather than reporting what it found; a summary that promises findings the body lacks; a summary with different numbers from the body.

## Status or progress report

**Reader's job:** know whether to worry and what is needed from them.

**Spine:**

1. Overall state in one line: health, the milestone reached, the biggest blocker or decision needed (or "none"), and what happens next, with the definition of the state words used (what "at risk" means here). If this line cannot be written, the situation is not yet understood. Pair each status word with a trend (improving, stable, declining); green means on track, not hopeful.
2. What changed since the last report, ahead of anything that did not.
3. Progress against plan: milestones with planned and actual or forecast dates; metrics with the target and baseline.
4. Risks and blockers, each with the effect on the plan, the owner, and the date by which a decision or action is needed.
5. Asks: each decision as a specific question with its options, the impact of each, a recommended option when the author has one, who decides, the date, and what happens if the date slips. If none are needed, say "No decisions needed this period."
6. Next period: the few things that will happen, not everything planned.

**Voice:** terse and factual. Prefer a short table to paragraphs for dates and owners. Do not list activity ("held meetings, reviewed items") in place of outcomes.

**Habits:** keep the same layout every period so the reader scans to what moved; mark each status with a word, never color alone; report a slip when you know about it, with its cause and recovery plan; keep hard failures separate from hunches.

**Traps:** all-green status over a late milestone; effort reported instead of results; unexplained changes in the plan baseline; asks buried in the last paragraph.

## Investor materials

Covers pitch deck, one-pager, investor memo, periodic investor update, accelerator or grant application answers, and the model excerpts that support them. This is writing support, not investment, legal, or financial advice.

Investor, customer or partner outreach messages (cold emails, pitches, sales follow-ups) are out of scope for all of the writing skills here; this section covers the materials, not the message that asks for a meeting.

**Golden rule: the materials must agree with each other.** Before drafting any piece, create or confirm one list of canonical facts: traction metrics, pricing and revenue assumptions, raise size and instrument, use of funds, team names and titles, milestones and dates. If drafts or assets disagree, stop and resolve it before writing more. Check every number in every asset against that list before sending.

**Workflow:** list canonical facts; list missing assumptions and log them as questions for the author in the reply; choose the asset; draft with the logic visible, stating each missing assumption plainly in the asset ("The notes give no churn figure for the downside case.") or leaving the claim out; cross-check every number.

**Default deck sequence** (a sensible order, not a rule; shorten only on purpose, never by burying the ask): company and wedge, problem, solution, product or demo, market, business model, traction, team, competition and differentiation, the ask, use of funds and milestones, appendix.

**One-pager or memo:** say what the company does in one clean sentence; say why now; put traction and proof early; make the ask precise; keep every claim easy to verify.

**Periodic investor update:** headline result and one-line state; key metrics against last period and plan with the same definitions each time; what went well and what did not, honestly; asks (introductions, hires, decisions); next-period commitments to be judged against later.

**Financial model excerpts:** show assumptions in plain view; layer revenue logic step by step; link spending to milestones; give bear, base, and bull cases where the decision depends on fragile assumptions, with sensitivity on those assumptions; do not attach probabilities to cases.

**Application answers:** answer the exact question asked; lead with traction, insight, and team advantage; no puffery; keep internal metrics identical to the deck and model.

**Red flags to fix before sending:** claims nobody can verify; market sizing with unstated assumptions; titles or roles that differ between assets; use-of-funds or revenue layers that do not sum exactly; certainty over fragile assumptions; figures without as-of dates. Label market-size and traction figures with their as-of date and verify current figures before anything goes outside.

**Pre-send gate:** every number matches the canonical list; sums are exact; assumptions are visible; the story is clear without hype; the piece could be defended line by line in a meeting.

## Post-mortem or incident review

This is the blameless document written for the team. A public blog post that tells the incident's story is `post-writing`.

**Reader's job:** learn what in the system allowed this, and agree on changes.

**Spine:**

1. Summary: what happened, impact (who, how many, how long, cost), and the main cause in plain terms, in a few sentences.
2. Impact: measured, with source. Separate what was measured from what is estimated.
3. Timeline: timestamped events with the information available at each point. Decisions are judged against what was known then. Times and durations are the author's; compute a duration only from given timestamps and show the arithmetic. Carry every value in the notes into the document (a config value and the value it should have had, a timeout, a threshold, a policy interval), because the cause is often one number.
4. Causes: the chain from trigger to impact, and the conditions that let it happen. Use why-questions until you reach something a change can fix, typically three to five levels. Each causal link has evidence (log, metric, measurement), or is marked as probable.
5. What went well and where luck helped. Luck is a finding too.
6. Action items: each tied to a verified cause and specific enough to check (name the system, threshold, document, or step; "improve monitoring" is not an action), with an owner, a date, and the evidence that will show it worked. Prefer changes to systems and checks over retraining or reminders. If the author gave no owner or date, the document says "Owner: to be assigned" and "Date: not set", and the question goes to the reply and `open-questions.md`; do not assign one, and do not leave a marker in the document.
7. Open questions and what is not known.

**Short incident (a page or less):** fold the spine into a summary paragraph that carries impact and main cause, one timeline, one causes passage and the follow-ups. Say each fact once: impact figures are not repeated under a second Impact heading, a timeline entry is not retold in the causes, and an unknown (for example why an alert was slow) is stated once where it matters and then only pointed to by the follow-up that investigates it. "Owner: to be assigned" and "Date: not set" are said once for the whole action list, not on every row.

**Blameless is a method, not a tone.** People are not the cause; the conditions in which a reasonable person acted that way are. Name roles and systems, not culprits, unless the people consented. If a statement ends at "human error," "carelessness," or "operator did not follow the procedure," keep going: why did the system allow, encourage, or fail to catch that? A root cause that restates the problem or whose only fix is "remind the team" is still a symptom.

**When the source notes blame a person,** keep the observable event (what was done, on what information, under what conditions) and drop the verdict ("X messed up", "careless"). Use the role, not the name, unless the person consented. Say in the change note that this was done.

**Separate confirmed, probable, and to fix.** Facts before opinions; cite the logs, frames, thresholds, or measurements. Do not infer actor or motive from the observable change; report the observable source. If a past event has no recoverable cause, say so.

**Voice:** calm, concrete, past tense for what happened and present for what we will change.

**Traps:** a timeline narrated with hindsight; a single root cause for a multi-factor event; action items with no owner or verification; a long apology with no mechanism; copying a template for a trivial event.

## Lessons-learned entry (short form)

A reusable record after hard or surprising work, not a diary line.

- Title names the pattern, not the event: "Config inheritance does not mean behavior inheritance across sessions", not "Fixed bug in payment module".
- Four to eight sentences: what happened in one sentence; the root cause (why-questions down to the mechanism); the class of problem it belongs to; the rule, phrased as "Next time I see this signal, I will take this action"; and the signal itself, specific enough to recognize.
- Before writing, search existing entries for the same root cause. Same cause, new symptom: add the symptom to the existing entry. New cause: new entry.
- Write one after debugging, rework, rollback, or a non-obvious decision. Skip trivial fixes. If the transferable sentence cannot be written, the pattern has not been extracted yet.
- Redact secrets and personal data before sharing.

## Audit or diagnostic report

When the report checks something against a standard and finds problems.

- Lead with what is broken and the verdict, not with what works. Split what is confirmed, what probably caused it, and what must be fixed. Keep it short.
- Findings table: location, evidence (quoted or measured), the issue, why it matters, confidence, action. Highest confidence first. Say how many findings there are by group.
- "Could not check X" is different from "X is broken." Do not call the audit complete if a required source failed to load; say what was not examined.
- If nothing is found, state the scope and assumptions and say the surface is clean in one line.
- Close with a clear verdict (pass or fail, or the needed fix).

## Gaps in a reader-facing report

A gap is something the notes do not say. The reader's document states it as a fact about the material, or omits the sentence; the author's question goes to the reply and `open-questions.md`. Never fill the gap with an estimate. Process narration ("as requested", "I checked with a scanner") and `[source: ...]` tags stay out of the document too.

| Gap | In the document (reader language) | Marker line, in the reply and `open-questions.md` |
|---|---|---|
| no recommendation in the notes | "The source analysis makes no recommendation. The evidence points toward A (analysis, not the author's decision); B would need (...)." Lead with that direction, then the options and a decision rule from the notes' own thresholds and deadlines | `[ASK AUTHOR: notes support A or B; which do you recommend?]` |
| no owner or date | "Owner: to be assigned." "Date: not set." | `[ASK AUTHOR: who owns the runbook update, and by when?]` |
| missing figure (cost, lift, baseline) | "The notes give no cost for this option." or omit the sentence | `[ASK AUTHOR: is there a cost estimate for option B?]` |
| cause not established | "The cause of the 7-minute alert delay is not known." | `[ASK AUTHOR: has the alert delay been investigated?]` |
| external fact to confirm | state what the notes say and that it is not confirmed beyond them | `[VERIFY: price quoted in the vendor note against the current list]` |
| command or query quoted but not run | omit it, or "This command was not run for this report." | `[UNTESTED: ...]` |

`open-questions.md` format (beside the saved report; skip it when nothing is open):

```markdown
# Open questions: <report title>

1. [ASK AUTHOR: notes support A or B; which do you recommend?]
   Blocks: Recommendation. The summary says: "The source analysis makes no recommendation."
2. [VERIFY: ...]
   Blocks: ...
```

List the same items in the reply, in full, with the same numbering.

Exception: the author asked for a draft to edit. Then the markers stay in the file at the gap, with a one-line draft banner at the top, and `open-questions.md` still exists.

Author-facing by nature, so markers may stay: the reply, `open-questions.md`, the revise-mode change list, and any worksheet or notes shown to the author before the report is sent.
