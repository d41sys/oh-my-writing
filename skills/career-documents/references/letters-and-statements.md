Load when: writing or revising a cover letter, letter of interest, statement of purpose, personal statement, motivation letter, or scholarship, fellowship or award essay.

# Letters and statements

Word counts, page counts and structure below are common practice. The program, employer, portal or funder's written instructions always win. Tell the user to re-read the current instructions before submitting, because limits and prompts change from year to year.

## 1. Choose the document

| Situation | Document |
| --- | --- |
| A formal posting asks for a letter, or the portal has a letter field | Cover letter (section 2) |
| No posting, interest in a function or team | Letter of interest, or a short outreach email (see profiles and outreach reference) |
| A named hiring manager is reached by cold or warm intro | Short email, not a pasted letter |
| Graduate or professional program, open prompt | Statement of purpose or personal statement (section 3) |
| European program or scholarship call | Motivation letter (section 3.6) |
| Award, scholarship, fellowship with published criteria | Criteria-mapped essay (section 4) |
| Public-sector post with listed selection criteria | Address each criterion under its own heading (section 2.8) |

Write the cover letter after the tailored résumé so the two agree. If only one upload is allowed, combine them in one file.

## 2. Cover letter

### 2.1 Job of the letter
A cover letter is a short argument that the candidate meets this employer's stated needs. It does not restate the résumé. It answers three questions the résumé cannot carry well: why this role, why this organization, and what two or three concrete experiences prove fit.

### 2.2 Before drafting
1. List three to five requirements from the posting and the two or three true achievements that answer them.
2. Find one organization-specific hook that is publicly checkable (a product, method, public project, stated problem in the posting). If none can be found, write without a fake one and tell the user in the reply that the letter has no organization-specific hook.
3. Write a one-sentence thesis of fit naming two or three true qualifications mapped to the requirements. The body proves it.
4. Check each fact against the candidate's material. A claim without support is removed from the letter; if it matters, the question goes to the reply as an `[ASK AUTHOR: ...]` line, never into the letter.

### 2.3 Structure (about three to four short paragraphs)
1. **Opening (two to three sentences):** name the role (and the requisition ID if the employer uses one), land a specific hook, and state the thesis. If the candidate has a direct link to the role (uses the product, knows someone who works there and has permission to name them, has deep field knowledge), put it first.
2. **Evidence paragraph one:** the strongest match. Their need, then the candidate's parallel experience, then the result or what was learned.
3. **Evidence paragraph two:** a second match or a different requirement. This is also the place for a short, honest gap note if one matters.
4. **Close (two to three sentences):** one specific contribution the candidate would bring, an invitation to talk, thanks, and any necessary logistics (relocation timing, pending credential date).

### 2.4 Openings
Hook types (use one true one): company-specific knowledge the candidate actually engaged with; a referral, with permission; a problem named in the posting paired with a short true parallel; one verified achievement that answers the primary need; a defensible observation about the market the employer serves.

Weak openings: "I am writing to apply for..." as the whole content; "I am the perfect candidate"; "I saw your posting on a job board" with nothing more; flattery with no checkable detail ("your innovative culture"); a stack of sentences all starting with "I".

Test: a reader who knows the company can tell which company and which role the opening belongs to within two sentences. Reread the opening as the reader and ask "so what?".

### 2.5 Evidence paragraphs
- Pick the two or three strongest matches. Do not tour the whole résumé.
- Add what the résumé cannot hold: constraints, stakeholders, trade-offs, why the result mattered.
- Skeleton: brief situation, what the candidate personally did, the result with a true figure if one exists.
- Match sentence shape: employer need, then parallel experience, then specific result.
- Tie each paragraph to a need the employer stated or a problem the research revealed. Explain how the experience carries into the new role.
- Show how a skill was used instead of claiming it.
- Attribution: every metric belongs to the employer and period it came from. Do not merge employers in one sentence. Past tense for ended roles. Use time words such as "earlier" or "later" only after checking the dates.
- Neither oversell nor undersell. State what was done. No self-verdicts, no apologetic set-ups.
- Break long paragraphs.

### 2.6 Gaps and complications
- **Skill gap:** acknowledge once, name what is being learned, point to the closest true proof.
- **Career change:** lead with transferable skills and bridge work; keep old titles out of the lead.
- **Employment gap:** one clean sentence of context (study, care, health, travel, layoff), then recent relevant activity.
- **Over-qualified:** say plainly why this scope is wanted.
- **Under-qualified or stretch:** adjacent scale, learning record, closest parallel; never invented years or titles.

### 2.7 Closing, tone and length
- Close on a named contribution and an easy next step. "Please find my résumé attached" is not a close. Promise a follow-up call only if the candidate will make it.
- Tone follows the employer: direct and concrete for startups and product tech; measured for large corporations; formal and criteria-explicit for public sector; some personality allowed in creative fields if still specific; mission linked to evidence in non-profits; formal with credentials and analysis early for finance and consulting.
- Length: a common range is about 250 to 400 words, under one page. Prefer the short end unless the sector expects fuller narrative. A portal limit or an employer's instruction overrides this.
- Sentence pass: break very long sentences, replace jargon with plain words, avoid starting consecutive sentences the same way, prefer active voice.
- Natural first person, confident and approachable, no template phrases ("aligns perfectly", "excited about the opportunity" as filler).

### 2.8 Format and regional conventions
- Salutation: a named person if findable, written exactly as the source gives it ("Dear Sam Whitfield"); a title (Ms, Mr, Dr) or a gender is added only when the source states it, because a guess from a first name can be wrong; otherwise "Dear Hiring Manager" or "Dear Search Committee" (or the team's own name, if the posting gives it). Never a bracket slot. Avoid "To Whom It May Concern".
- Sign-off by region: US usually "Sincerely" or "Best regards"; UK "Kind regards", and "Yours sincerely" with a named recipient or "Yours faithfully" with "Dear Sir or Madam"; Australia and New Zealand "Kind regards", with "Cheers" only once rapport exists and the organization is informal.
- Full business-letter block (date, recipient, address, RE line) when the letter is a separate document; omit the address block when the letter is the body of an email or a portal text. Add a reference line with the requisition ID for large employers.
- Email submission: subject with name and role; attach or paste as the instructions say; mention attachments; proofread the address and subject.
- File name: name and "Cover Letter".
- Public-sector criteria letters: put each stated criterion as a heading and map one evidence item under it, in the employer's order. Do not force a three-paragraph shape.
- Sector emphasis: technical roles name real technologies and public work; marketing names channels and measured effects; finance leads with credentials and analytical rigor; startups value initiative and scrappiness when evidenced; large organizations value process and scale when evidenced.

### 2.9 Delivery note
After the letter give: which achievements were chosen and why, any gap handled, company facts used (with where the user can verify them), and optional alternative openings.

## 3. Statements of purpose, personal statements, motivation letters

### 3.1 Obey the prompt
Programs reuse labels loosely. Read the verbs in the prompt (research interests, personal qualities, career goals, contribution) and answer exactly those. Assign every question in the prompt a paragraph; an unanswered question is a defect.

| Genre | Center of gravity | Reader's question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Statement of purpose | research or academic trajectory, preparation, proposed direction | Can this person do the work here and finish? |
| Personal statement | path, motivation, relevant experience, fit for a field or profession | Who is this applicant and why this path? |
| Motivation letter | goals after the degree, why this program, why now, contribution | Why this program, why this person, to what end? |
| Letter of intent | prompt-defined | What does the call ask? |

For research doctorates, write as a future colleague: preparation, a research direction showing how the applicant thinks, evidence of finishing power, and program fit. It is not a memoir. The sketched project is not a binding promise. For professional and many master's programs, motivation, relevant experience and qualities shown through incidents, plus fit.

### 3.2 What to gather
Ask the user only for what the prompt needs: related work or research; defining experiences; how interest in the field began and grew; accomplishments they are proud of; people and ideas that influenced them; why this program; what they would add to the cohort; near-term and longer-term goals; anything that explains a record the file will show. Also ask for the program details to cite: courses, methods, labs, centers, clinics, archives, and named faculty with a recent paper or project.

### 3.3 Structures
General personal statement: hook (a specific scene, problem or intellectual moment, not a résumé recap) then evidence paragraphs (courses, research, work, leadership) with topic sentences and takeaways, then why this program and where the applicant is headed.

Research statement (scale to the stated limit): who the applicant is as a scholar and the research focus in a few lines; current or recent advanced work as preparation; the research plan and what question it addresses; relevant background filtered by usefulness to that plan; fit with the program, one or two faculty, and facilities; an optional short close that is first to go when over length. Many strong statements move present work, then future plan, then past evidence, then fit, rather than starting with childhood. An origin story is fine when it is tied to the research agenda.

Teaching interests: include when the program values teaching; otherwise keep them secondary.

Master's example shape: a hook from a problem met at work; two evidence blocks (a methods course and an internship result); the goal for the degree; fit with two curriculum elements and a lab; a short close on contribution.

Aids for the reader: strong topic sentences, the main claim at paragraph edges, causal transitions ("that result led me to join X lab") instead of additive ones, and one theme that runs through and links opening and close. State the objective early, within the first two paragraphs. After drafting, outline what was written and check that each paragraph has a reason for its position and adds something no other paragraph does.

### 3.4 Fit paragraph
- Name the program correctly. Link specific curriculum, methods, placements, labs or archives to the applicant's stated aims.
- Mention typically one or two faculty whose current work matches. Cite something recent (a paper, a method, a project). Say what the applicant would learn or contribute. Do not imply a relationship or commitment the person has not made. Name mentors only when accurate and ideally consistent with recommendations.
- If there is no project yet, name the directions of interest and the expertise the applicant wants to acquire.
- Goals: honest and scaled. Not empty ("find myself") and not out of proportion with the path.
- Uniqueness test: if hundreds of other applicants could copy the sentence unchanged, replace it with an operational detail. Delete the university name: if the fit paragraph still works, it is not fit.
- Preparation: state languages, methods and grade context truthfully. A recent weak grade may get one plain explanation when it informs readiness.
- The user must check faculty and program details against the current department pages. Use them in the statement only as the user supplied them, and list each in the reply as a `[VERIFY: ...]` line when they came from memory; the statement carries no marker or "to be checked" note.

### 3.5 What to avoid
- Clichés ("always dreamed", "passion for helping", "make a difference") with no scene behind them.
- Opening with a famous quotation, which hands the first words to someone else.
- Flattery of rank or reputation.
- Long unexplained grievance. A weakness gets a brief, factual, forward-looking line.
- Repeating the CV without interpretation. Tell the committee something the file does not already show.
- Hyperbole and jargon meant to impress. Discipline-specific terms are fine for an expert reader when used accurately.
- Showing traits by naming them. Show them through an incident.
- Hardship used as spectacle. Include it when the prompt invites it or it is needed context, kept proportionate and tied to readiness.
- Writing what the committee is assumed to want instead of what is true.

### 3.6 European motivation letters
These often ask: the professional goal and sector after the degree; the contribution the applicant intends to make once in a position to act (grounded in experience or credible facts, not slogans); why this program, naming courses, modules or methods; why this candidate, with proof of roles and strengths; and, for some country-specific calls, why this country and what its system or sector offers, only with real research. Write continuous prose, not the questionnaire; keep the guiding questions and the outline out of the final letter. Be reflective and factual, not a campaign speech or a résumé. State strengths plainly without false modesty. Length varies: some calls ask for one page, others for two or three. If the call is silent, use the shorter safe default and say so. Check the current call text. Never invent a contact, an NGO role or a technology-transfer plan.

### 3.7 Length and revision
- The stated limit (words, characters, pages, box size) always wins. If none, roughly one to two pages is the common starting point for many graduate statements. Do not squeeze with small type or margins.
- If over the limit, cut the ornamental close first, then repeated biography, then duplicate evidence. Keep preparation, plan or goals, and fit.
- Character-limited portals reward a long draft compressed with names, methods and results kept.
- Revise over several drafts spread across days, not one evening. Proofread the final version for wrong-word errors a spell checker misses.
- Get readers: someone who knows the applicant, someone who can critique the writing, and someone in the field. The user arranges this; offer it as a step.
- Questions while revising: is this compelling, is this memorable, could anyone else have written it.
- Positive tone: turn a complaint into the hope behind it when that is true.

### 3.8 Several programs
Each program needs its own version. Swapping a faculty name is not tailoring. Keep a short list of what is shared (story, preparation) and what is per-program (fit, plan emphasis, prompt answers).

## 4. Award, scholarship and fellowship essays

1. **Scorecard:** these are marked against published criteria. A missed criterion is a structural failure.
2. **Capture first:** award name, organizer, criteria wording, per-section and total limits, any weighting, first-time or resubmission, and the applicant's achievements.
3. **Map before prose:** criterion, achievement, evidence or figure, short example. Flag empty rows early.
4. **Write in the form's criterion order** so scorers do not hunt. Each criterion section stands alone: brief situation, concrete action, quantified result.
5. **Lead with the impact.** First sentence carries the strongest true result. Origins and mission take one line at most.
6. **Quantify for comparison:** counts, time saved, people served, revenue, retention. Approximations need the applicant's confirmation and qualifiers ("about", "more than").
7. **Replace adjectives with mechanisms and outcomes.** "Innovative" becomes what was built, how, and what changed.
8. **Hard limits:** over-length entries may be discarded. Use the space with substance. When cutting: generic set-up first, then merge parts, then hedges; keep one strong example over three weak ones.
9. **Context:** first-time applicants win on specificity; small organizations may stress growth or per-person impact; resubmissions answer every prior feedback point and add new evidence.
10. **If evidence is thin:** say what exists and the trajectory. Do not invent. Published judging guides and past winners can show what evaluators value.
11. **Do not copy the same text** across categories or awards.

## 5. Checks before returning any letter or statement

1. Every named fact traces to the candidate's material or a source the user supplied.
2. The organization or program name cannot be swapped without breaking a sentence.
3. At least one specific, true result appears.
4. Every prompt question or criterion has a home.
5. Length is verified against the governing limit, not against these heuristics.
6. Clichés and template phrases replaced by evidence, in the author's voice.
7. Regional salutation, sign-off and spelling consistent.
8. The statement or letter itself holds no marker, bracket placeholder or note to the author; open items and `[VERIFY: ...]` lines are in the reply (and in `open-questions.md` when a file is written).

## 6. Gaps in a letter or statement

The reader is a hiring manager or a committee. A gap is stated as a fact about the candidate's record, in one plain sentence, or the sentence is left out; the question goes to the author.

| Gap | In the letter or statement | In the reply and `open-questions.md` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| no result or figure for an example | the action and what is known to have happened, without a number | `[ASK AUTHOR: how many users, and what changed after the migration?]` |
| no fit detail about the employer or program | a shorter fit paragraph naming only what the user supplied or the posting states; no flattery filler | `[ASK AUTHOR: one course, lab or project at the program that matters to you, and why?]` |
| no faculty or lab the user has read | directions of interest and the expertise wanted, no named person | `[ASK AUTHOR: a faculty member whose current work you have read?]` |
| prompt question with no material (a failure, a leadership example) | omitted, or one plain sentence on the situation as it is ("My thesis is my first research project.") | `[ASK AUTHOR: is there an example for the prompt on teamwork?]` |
| a program fact from memory (deadline, limit, course name) | used only as the user stated it; no hedge in the text | `[VERIFY: course name against the current curriculum page]` |
| weak grade or career gap that informs readiness | one factual line, dates accurate | none unless the cause is unknown: `[ASK AUTHOR: one-line reason for the gap in 2022?]` |

Exception: the user asked for a draft to edit. Then markers sit in the text at the gap and the reply says it is a draft. Process material stays out of the letter in every case: no per-paragraph source notes, no "this paragraph answers prompt 2", no outline or guiding questions (a European motivation letter is continuous prose, not the questionnaire). An outline or requirement map shown to the user beside the letter is author-facing.
