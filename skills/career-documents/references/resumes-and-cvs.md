Load when: building, tailoring, shortening or checking a résumé or academic CV, writing accomplishment bullets, handling numbers, career change, gaps, or parse-safe formatting.

# Résumés and CVs

Length figures, section orders and file-type advice below are common practice drawn from career-center style guidance. They differ by region, field and employer. Name the norm you are using and tell the user to check the target's instructions.

## 1. Pick the document type

| Question | If yes | If no |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does the posting or program ask for a "CV" and is the role scholarly (faculty, postdoc, research fellowship, many graduate applications)? | Academic CV: complete scholarly record, section 9 | Continue |
| Is the role in industry, public service, or non-academic research? | Résumé: selective, tailored, short | Ask what the target wants |
| Is "CV" used in the user's country as the name for an ordinary job document? | Treat as a résumé; follow local length and content norms | Keep the split above |

A researcher applying to industry sends a selected, outcome-focused résumé, not the full publication list. Move the full list behind a link only when the reader asks for it.

## 2. Truth contract for every line

1. Employers, titles, dates, degrees, certifications, publications, grants, tools, languages and skills come from the candidate.
2. Do not enlarge scope. "Met revenue targets" is not "owned the P&L". "Contributed to product definition" is not product ownership. Do not add team size, budget, company stage, customer type or business model unless the candidate stated it.
3. Numbers come from the candidate. Estimates need confirmation, a note of how they were derived, and a conservative bias.
4. Tailoring is selection, ordering and wording. It never changes a title, a date or a tool.
5. Ambiguity defaults to weaker wording or omission, and is listed for the candidate to resolve.
6. Interview test: the candidate must be able to explain every listed tool, project and figure in depth. If not, cut it or ask.
7. Ground the draft in a supplied résumé or work-history notes, not in general knowledge about the candidate or the company.

When instructions conflict, use this priority: accuracy, then the candidate's corrections, then the target's instructions, then writing quality, then format, then tone.

## 3. Section order by stage

Reverse-chronological order inside Experience and Education is the default. Put the evidence that best proves fit highest.

| Stage | Typical order | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Student or early career | Contact, short summary (optional), Education, Experience (internships, part-time, research, leadership), Projects, Skills | Education can lead while experience is thin. Include GPA only if strong and customary for the field. Drop secondary school after the first year or so of higher education. |
| Mid-career | Contact, summary, Experience, Skills, Education, Certifications | Coursework drops out once experience replaces it. |
| Senior or executive | Contact, executive profile, career highlights, Experience with company context, board or advisory, Education | Scope claims (budget, headcount, P&L) only if true. Years on degrees may be omitted where age bias is a concern; follow the candidate's wish and local norms. |
| Career changer | Contact, summary that explains the move, transferable skills, reframed Experience, Bridge experience, Education | See section 7. |

**Contact block:** name, professional email, phone, city and region. Add profile or portfolio links when relevant. Put it in the page body, not only in a header or footer, because some parsers skip those areas. Leave out street address, photo, date of birth, marital status and national ID numbers unless the market expects them (section 11).

**Summary versus objective:** use one or neither. A summary states identity, years or domain, one signature proof, and optionally the target. An objective names the role sought. Skip it when space is tight and the path is linear. Use it for career changes, returns to work, specialized fits, or long careers. Cut hollow openers such as "seeking a challenging position" or "dynamic team player".

**Skills:** list tools and methods the candidate can use with little supervision, grouped when the list is long (for example languages, frameworks, cloud, domain). Mirror the posting's wording where true. Soft skills belong in bullets as evidence, not as a list of adjectives. Leave out trivial defaults and anything the candidate could not discuss. Proficiency labels only when honest. Skill bars and star ratings are subjective and often parse badly.

**Experience entry:** organization, location, title, dates in one consistent format, then bullets. Several titles at one employer: list the employer once with nested roles. Freelance: one banner with a "clients include" line when accurate. Short tenures: keep if relevant and frame by what was delivered; do not apologize on the page.

**Projects:** for early-career, career-change, technical or portfolio-led candidates. Title, dates or context, two to three bullets on problem, depth and outcome. Prefer real users and substantial work over tutorial copies.

**Education:** degree names as the institution writes them, concentration, expected date if in progress. Thesis title, fellowship or relevant coursework only when they strengthen fit.

**Volunteer and unpaid work:** if it relates to the target, list it in Experience with dates. Keep a separate Activities or Volunteer section for ongoing, meaningful involvement outside the career goal. List single events nowhere.

**Languages:** say how each is used (reading, writing, speaking) with wording matching the posting.

**Leave out by default:** "references available on request", salary expectations, reasons for leaving, hobbies unless relevant, and old jobs superseded by later work (a common window is the last 10 to 15 years of relevant work unless an early role is uniquely probative).

## 4. Accomplishment bullets

A bullet shows what the candidate did and what it led to. Lines such as "Responsible for", "Helped with", "Assisted in", "Participated in" and "Worked on" show proximity, not contribution.

**Formulas (pick one and keep it consistent in a pass):**

- Action + object + scope or context + result.
- "Accomplished X, measured by Y, by doing Z."
- Challenge, action, result, compressed into one line.
- Technical: action + what was built or changed + scale + stack + effect.

**Density:** one or two lines per bullet. Recent roles carry more bullets than older ones; the most recent role may have five to seven, older roles two to three. Do not pad. Not every bullet needs a number. A true qualitative result ("adopted as the team's standard review checklist") beats an invented figure.

**The "so what" test:** after the bullet, ask what changed because of this work. If nothing is stated, find out: what was faster, cheaper, safer, larger, more accurate? What would have happened without the candidate?

**Verbs:** match the verb to the real contribution: led, directed, mentored; built, launched, designed; reduced, streamlined, rebuilt; analyzed, evaluated; presented, negotiated; migrated, automated. Vary openers. Do not use "led" for work done as a participant.

**Metric families to ask about:**

| Kind | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Money | revenue, cost, budget, deal size |
| Percent and ratio | growth, error rate, conversion, with a baseline |
| Time | cycle time, hours saved, schedule, response time |
| Volume and scale | users, tickets, transactions, team size, data volume |
| Quality | satisfaction, accuracy, uptime, audit result |
| Frequency | per day, week, month |
| Rank or comparison | position in a group, before and after, against a team average |

Role prompts: sales (quota attainment, pipeline, retention); marketing (leads, engagement, conversion); support (tickets, response time, resolution rate); operations (throughput, cost, error rate); engineering (latency, uptime, deploy frequency, coverage); project work (count, budget, on-time rate, stakeholders); administration or HR (hires, time to fill, training completion). Use these as questions to the candidate, never as values.

**Finding numbers without inventing them.** A request such as "make it more impressive with numbers" is not a source of numbers. When the candidate has supplied none, do not add any: keep the bullet's true content, mark the missing figure on the line (`[ASK AUTHOR: how many tickets a week, and from what baseline?]`), and give a short list of the questions to answer. A placeholder range or a typical figure for the role is still invention.

1. Ask scale, frequency, before and after, and who benefited.
2. If outcomes are confidential or unknown, quantify what the candidate controlled: interviews run, items processed, pages written, people trained.
3. If a figure can be derived, show the derivation to the candidate (five client meetings a week for fifty weeks is about 250 a year) and mark it as an estimate for confirmation.
4. Round down. A remembered "about half" becomes "roughly 40%" only if the candidate agrees.
5. For team results, state the candidate's share only if they confirm it.
6. Entry-level work can still carry numbers on volume, accuracy and speed of learning.

**Number hygiene:** at most two or three numbers per bullet; every percentage with a baseline or context; no figure that reveals confidential data, that the candidate cannot explain, or that makes them look worse without need. When absolute values are confidential, use percentages or ranges the candidate may share. If a bullet is too long, keep the most meaningful number and cut the rest.

**Failure modes:** passive voice; no result; vague nouns ("stakeholders", "processes"); a stack of three claims in one bullet; trailing "demonstrating leadership" or "showcasing expertise" clauses; every bullet in the same shape; adjective chains; "leveraging" as a prefix. Fix by moving the verb to the front, splitting the bullet, or replacing the tail with the real result.

**Quantified-bullet note (give to the user when numbers were added):** the figure, where it came from, whether it is exact or estimated, and what is still unknown.

Examples (invented; replace with the candidate's facts):

- "Handled customer complaints" becomes "Resolved escalated complaints for a regional clinic network; cut repeat contacts by tracking causes in a shared log" if the candidate confirms the log and the effect. Without the effect: "Resolved escalated complaints for a regional clinic network and kept a shared log of causes."
- "Built dashboards" becomes "Built a weekly sales dashboard used by 8 regional managers, replacing a manual spreadsheet that took half a day to compile" if all details are supplied.

## 5. Tailoring to a posting

1. **Prerequisites.** A base résumé is required. A work-history file (the evidence inventory) is strongly preferred. If it is missing, list every assumption you make about company, scope and tools, and ask the candidate to confirm before treating the draft as final.
2. **Parse the posting** into: must-have requirements (education, years, licenses, core skills), preferred items, day-to-day responsibilities, tools and domain terms, level (individual contributor, manager, executive), and stated team context. Read "required", "must" and a Requirements heading as must-have; "preferred", "ideally", "a plus" as preferred. A term repeated several times often carries more weight; treat that as a hint, not a rule.
3. **Map each requirement** to direct, adjacent or transferable evidence, or to a gap. Rank gaps as critical (possible deal-breaker), major (address in the letter), minor (let related strength carry it).
4. **Dealbreakers** to raise before any writing: a required license or clearance the candidate lacks, a required degree not held, a location or travel requirement the candidate cannot meet, or experience far below the stated level. Slightly fewer years, a missing preferred degree, or a learnable tool are usually not deal-breakers. The candidate decides. A required or preferred tool, language or credential that is absent from the candidate's material stays out of Skills, summary and bullets, including softened as "familiar with", "exposure to" or "working knowledge", unless the candidate confirms real use at that level. Put the nearest true evidence in its own words (a related tool, a course, a project) and list the gap in the note; one plain line on learning is allowed only if the candidate says it is true.
5. **Fit reading.** A rough count of must-haves met is a conversation aid. Do not present a percentage as a prediction. Many matches may hint at over-qualification, which the letter should address; few matches are a stretch.
6. **Throughline.** Write a two or three sentence account of why this person is a strong candidate for this role, using only supported claims. Let it steer the summary and the choice of bullets.
7. **Edit in this order:**
   1. Summary rewritten for the role: the central terms in natural prose, short sentences, no empty adjectives.
   2. Skills reordered to lead with the posting's priorities; add only terms that are true.
   3. Each role led by its two most relevant bullets; pull other true bullets from the master; demote or drop low-relevance lines.
   4. Posting terms used where they describe the work. If the résumé says "risk mitigation" and the posting says "risk management" about the same work, use the posting's term. If the work differs, keep the candidate's term.
   5. Keep all roles unless dropping one hides nothing; compress older roles rather than leaving suspicious gaps.
   6. Trim to the chosen length without shrinking type or margins below readability.
8. **Keyword placement.** Central terms may appear in the summary, the skills list and inside bullets with context. Repeating them across these places is normal; piling them up is stuffing. Spell out an acronym on first use.
9. **Level calibration.** Executive wording stresses strategy and scope; a director balances program ownership and leadership; an individual contributor stresses hands-on depth and deliverables. Do not dress an individual-contributor history as executive.
10. **Preserve the candidate's structure.** Use the headings the original résumé uses. Add sections only when agreed.
11. **Version discipline.** One master; copy for each application; name versions by role and organization and date; keep a tracker (organization, role, version, date, channel, status); archive rather than delete. Update the master after promotions, finished projects, new credentials, and on a regular refresh.
12. **Pre-submit read.** Summary names the role or function; top skills match top requirements; the first bullet of each role answers a key requirement; every claim is true; titles and dates match the master.
13. **Change report.** Show summary before and after, skill order changes, bullets moved or rewritten (before and after), terms added and where, and anything dropped.

Volume note: a few deeply tailored applications to roles that match well usually serve the candidate better than many identical sends. Offer this as advice, not as a quota.

## 6. Level of reader and scenarios

- Technical role at a non-technical employer: lead with technical work and attach the business effect to each bullet.
- Moving from individual contributor to management: lead with team-leading bullets, keep some technical ones for credibility.
- Large company to startup: show initiative, breadth and work done with little structure. Startup to large company: show process improvement, scale and cross-team work.
- Internal posting: use internal knowledge, cross-team relationships and named initiatives.
- Reposted role: apply if interested, and ask whether the requirements changed.

## 7. Career change, gaps, short tenures

**Translation:** keep the real action and result; replace local nouns with target-field terms only when the mapping is honest. Examples of honest mappings: lesson plans to training curriculum; unit to team; guests to customers. A retail schedule is not P&L ownership. Teaching one class is not an enterprise training program.

**Summary bridge pattern:** target-field identity, years of transferable strength, one specific advantage from the prior field, and three true capabilities.

**Structure options:** chronological with rewritten bullets when the bridge is clear; a hybrid with transferable skill headings plus a short chronological list when titles mislead. A functional-only layout that hides employers and dates tends to raise suspicion; use only with the candidate's informed choice.

**Bridge experience:** volunteer work, freelance projects, certifications, coursework, side projects, professional communities in the target field. List them with dates and label them accurately.

**Why the switch (for the letter or an interview, not the résumé):** a short, forward-looking reason: interest that grew, a specific experience that sparked it, skills that carry over, what the new path offers. Do not put burnout, dislike of the old job, or pay as the motive on paper.

**Employment gap:** keep dates accurate; never hide or invent. Use years-only dates when month precision exaggerates a gap and nothing is falsified. Mention real activity in the gap (study, freelance, caregiving, volunteering, projects). One sentence in the cover letter is enough.

**Skill gap:** close it with real courses or projects and list them under Skills or Coursework. Never claim unearned mastery.

## 8. Variants

**Technical (software, data, infrastructure, technical product):**
- Skills high on the page, grouped, ordered by relevance to the posting. Leave out skill meters and one-time tools.
- Bullets: what was built or changed, scale (users, requests, data volume, uptime), stack, effect.
- Projects matter most for juniors, career changers, bootcamp graduates and people with gaps. Prefer work with real users or substantial open-source contribution.
- Put code-hosting and portfolio links in the contact line, and also in the Skills section if the parser ignores headers.
- Use only repositories the candidate can explain.

**Executive:**
- Profile that states function, scope and what the person is known for, followed by a handful of career highlights, then Experience with company context (size, stage, scope).
- Bullets: leadership action, strategic initiative, outcome at scale, before and after. Budget, headcount and P&L only if true. Use percentages or ranges when absolute figures are confidential.
- Often two pages; three only for very senior or board-facing documents. Skip first-person pronouns and task lists.

**Creative (design, content, brand, media):**
- Two versions: a parse-safe one for portals and a designed one for portfolios and direct sends. The designed one still needs clear hierarchy and a readable first screen.
- The portfolio carries the proof. Add case-study links under relevant bullets.
- Keep the content identical in both versions.

**Student or recent graduate:** Education, relevant projects, leadership, coursework and part-time work all count as evidence. Show skills in bullets across sections, not only in a list. Include real career-readiness evidence (teamwork, communication, problem solving) as accomplishments.

## 9. Academic CV

**Purpose:** a comprehensive scholarly record for faculty, research and postdoctoral posts, fellowships and many graduate programs. It grows with the career and has no page cap. Tailor the order, not the completeness.

**Typical stack (reorder by target):** contact with profile identifier (such as ORCID) if used; Education with advisor and thesis title; academic and research appointments; publications; presentations; grants and funding; teaching; mentoring; service; memberships; honors; references only if the field expects them.

**Reorder by job family:** research posts lead with publications, funding and research appointments; teaching posts lead with teaching, course design and evaluations; administrative posts lead with leadership and service. Postdoc and research-scientist applications foreground publications, technical skills, collaborations and future research potential.

**Publications:** the candidate's name in bold or otherwise marked; the citation style normal in the field; group by type (journal articles, books and chapters, proceedings, under review, in preparation, if the field accepts these lists); DOIs when available; mark student co-authors if customary. Never invent acceptance, author order or venue. Many sciences number entries and care about author position; humanities weight books and sole-authored work.

**Grants:** agency or mechanism, role (principal, co-principal, co-investigator), title, dates, total and the candidate's share when split. Pending items follow local custom.

**Teaching and mentoring:** course title and number, role, term, enrollment, new preparations; mentees with status and placements when accurate. List guest lectures separately from owned courses.

**Presentations:** split invited talks, conference talks (marked oral or poster), campus talks, and public outreach.

**Service:** group by level: profession (reviewing, editorial roles, organizing), university, department.

**Length by stage** (illustrative; do not pad): graduate student a few pages; postdoc several; early faculty roughly five to ten; senior can be much longer. Check regional norms; follow the department's template over any generic one.

**Final CV check:** consistent formatting, reverse-chronological order (unless the field orders publications otherwise), no unexplained gaps, updated recently, and nothing listed that the candidate cannot document.

## 10. Parse-safe format and file handling

Many employers run applications through parsing software. Behavior varies by vendor, so favor clean structure over chasing quirks, and verify on the target portal when possible.

1. **File:** follow the posting. Otherwise a word-processor file or a text-based PDF exported from a word processor is the common safe choice for portals; a PDF suits direct email to a person. Avoid scanned images and design-tool exports that parsers read badly. Use a clear file name with the candidate's name and role.
2. **Layout for portal versions:** single column; no text boxes, layout tables, icons, photos, charts, skill bars; essential contact data in the body; standard bullet characters; plain horizontal rules at most.
3. **Fonts and spacing:** common fonts at body size around 10 to 12 points; name largest; consistent date placement; margins roughly half an inch to an inch; fix a half-empty page with relevant true content, not with wide margins.
4. **Headings:** standard names (Experience, Education, Skills, Summary, Projects, Certifications). Creative headings may be skipped by parsers.
5. **Consistency:** one date format; one tense convention (past for ended roles, present for the current one); bold used for names, titles and headings only.
6. **Plain-text test:** copy the text into a plain editor. Reading order should be coherent and no section should disappear. Report problems as current state and recommended change.
7. **Dual audience:** a human may glance at the page for seconds. The name, current fit, and strongest recent proof must be visible without decoding graphics. Treat any "seconds" figure as a design target.
8. **Letter match:** use the same header and font family for the cover letter.
9. **References:** kept on a separate page, offered when asked, not on the résumé.

## 11. Length and regional conventions

Verify with the employer or a local source. A common pattern, as a starting point only:

| Element | US | UK | Australia and New Zealand |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Usual name | résumé | CV for industry; separate academic CV | CV or résumé |
| Typical length | one page common for students and early career; two for experienced | about two | about two to three |
| Photo, date of birth | omit | omit | omit |
| Nationality, visa | usually omit | state if relevant | often state when relevant |
| Referees | omit; separate list on request | omit | omit or only if asked |
| Address | city and state | city | city and state |

Other regions follow different customs (a photo, birth date or marital status is normal in some countries). Do not assume. Ask the user for the country and field, or tell them to check a local source or the employer.

Decision rule for length: start from region, seniority and reader (industry or academic); keep one page until relevant, recent evidence needs more; use more than two pages only for the document types that expect it; never pad with duties.

## 12. Final assembly checklist

1. Integrity: no invented facts; estimates confirmed; titles and dates match the master.
2. Type and length: right document; length band named and met.
3. Structure: order fits stage and variant; contact complete; every section earns its lines.
4. Bullets: active verbs; each carries scope or result; one to two lines; varied openers.
5. Parse: single column for portals; standard headings; text-based file; plain-text test passed.
6. Tailoring: requirements mapped; terms true; summary and top bullets show fit; version named and logged.
7. Special cases: career-change wording; creative pair; academic completeness.
8. Human pass: spelling, tense, links, phone and email, confidential details removed, and a trusted reader (mentor, counselor, colleague) has seen it before sending.
