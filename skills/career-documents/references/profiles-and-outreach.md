Load when: writing a LinkedIn or portfolio profile, a portfolio case study, a story bank, short application-form answers, a cold or networking email, a reference request, an offer reply, or when the English locale of a message must be chosen.

# Profiles, portfolios, form answers and outreach

Character limits, field sizes and platform features named below change. Treat them as snapshots to verify in the live interface. Facts, titles, dates and numbers must match the résumé; expand wording, never history.

## 1. Professional network profile

A profile is a long-lived public record read by many recruiters and peers. It differs from a résumé (one application) and from feed posts (episodic; use `post-writing`).

| | Résumé | Profile |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audience | one employer | search and network |
| Voice | compact, employer-specific | conversational-professional, industry-wide terms |
| Length | one to two pages | long-form, with per-field caps |
| Update | per application | kept current |

**Headline:** the search surface. Skeleton: role or target role, domain or stack or method, concrete value or scope. Replace status slogans ("looking for opportunities", bare "student at ...") with a craft signal. Use phrases recruiters actually type, only where true. Example shape (invented): "Backend engineer | Distributed systems, Go | Reliability for payment APIs". Verify the current character cap before cutting.

**About:** only the opening lines show before "see more", so the first sentence must carry a concrete claim, not throat-clearing. A workable skeleton in three to five short paragraphs:
1. A hook line: who the person helps or what they build.
2. Trajectory: years, domains, one or two verified scope anchors.
3. "What I do best" as real work modes, not trait adjectives.
4. Current and prior context in plain sentences.
5. A skills line with true skills.
6. A low-friction way to reach the person.

Failure modes: generic passion claims; keyword walls with no projects; invented revenue, headcount or "large company" prestige; pasting the résumé without voice.

**Experience entries:** title, organization, dates, a two or three sentence frame, then a few evidence bullets. Entries may carry more context and links than the résumé. Keep team credit explicit ("with a team of five", "individual contributor on ..."). Titles and dates equal the résumé.

**Skills and featured items:** order by relevance to target roles. Feature work the person can defend: a talk, an article, a demo, a case study. Skills only if true.

**Recommendations:** ask specific people after real shared outcomes, one at a time. Offer talking points. Never ghost-write praise or invent endorsements.

**Keywords:** collect terms from target postings, from profiles of people in the target role, and from industry publications. Place them where true: headline, About, experience, skills. A term the person has not used is fabrication, however useful for search.

**Work-status banners and "open to work" settings:** state true titles, locations and job types; choose visibility deliberately.

**Delivery shape for a profile review:** current versus proposed headline; full proposed About; per-role changes; skills to add or reorder; terms integrated; a short action list. Do not give a "completeness score" or "search score" unless a real tool produced it.

Snapshot caution: figures such as headline length, About length, number of skills, recommendation counts and "profiles with X get N times the views" circulate as folklore. Use them as motivation at most. Never present them as platform policy.

**Consistency audit:** no invented employers or numbers; no keyword stuffing; the profile and résumé do not contradict; confidential figures are redacted.

## 2. Portfolio case studies

A résumé bullet says what happened; a case study shows how and why, with honest role boundaries. Common for product, design, content and many engineering roles.

**Spine, in order:**
1. Quick facts: project, organization, the candidate's role, timeline, team, one-line impact.
2. Problem: stakes, baseline, constraints, the goal if one was set.
3. Process: research, options considered with the criteria used, decisions and reasons, who was involved.
4. Solution: what shipped, mapped back to the problems.
5. Results: numbers with baseline and timeframe, secondary effects, and what did not move. Compare against the stated goal when there was one.
6. Learnings: what worked, what would change, skills built.

**Emphasis by role:** product (prioritization, trade-offs, metric definition); design (research methods, artifacts, usability evidence, before and after); engineering (problem framing, architecture, failure modes, performance or reliability proof); marketing (audience, channel, creative, honest attribution, return only when measured).

**Integrity:** say who did what ("I designed ...", "I co-led discovery with ..."). Do not absorb team results as solo work. Blur or omit confidential figures, customer names and unreleased plans, and mark redactions. Prefer ranges or relative change when absolute numbers are restricted. Never invent test lifts, revenue or headcount.

**Visuals:** before and after, key screens or diagrams, simple results tables. Caption each, size them consistently, check legibility on a phone, strip anything private.

**Length:** a quick-read path of a few minutes with an optional deeper layer for interviewers. Choose one project the candidate can defend at length.

**Do not publish** a case study whose proof depends on private screens, secrets, customer data or claims the candidate cannot document.

## 3. Story bank (reusable evidence)

A written set of true episodes reused in interviews, form answers and profile bullets.

**Spine (situation, task, action, result):** context in one or two sentences; the candidate's ownership in one; what the candidate did in two or three, with decisions and tools; the result with a number or a true qualitative outcome, plus why it mattered. Keep three lengths of each story from the same facts: full, a shorter cut, and a one-line version. Figures never grow as the version shrinks.

**Coverage:** leadership, problem solving, collaboration across functions or without authority, achievement under pressure, and a real failure with the change it caused. Aim for enough distinct stories to cover what the target role tests, not a fixed count. Index each story to the résumé bullets it expands.

**Difficult prompts (still fact-bound):**
- Weakness: a real limitation, how it shows up, the habit that offsets it. No disguised strengths.
- Why leaving: brief, forward-looking, no criticism of the employer.
- Failure: the actual miss, what was learned, what changed and evidence of it.
- Tell me about yourself: present role, relevant past, why this target; end on what the candidate wants next.

**Stress test:** could the candidate answer follow-ups about alternatives, tools, and personal versus team credit?

## 4. Short application answers

Form fields want a direct answer, not a cover letter in a box. Read the posting, the candidate's material and the exact prompt. If either is missing, ask.

| Field type | Aim | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Years with a skill | honest duration plus one anchor | "[Skill], [N] years. Used for [project or context]. Also [adjacent tool]." |
| Why this company | specific to this employer, short | a concrete public fact, then the candidate's real related work, then an optional motive; do not restate the posting |
| Links or portfolio | scannable list | name, link, one line on what and one proof; most relevant first |
| Self-rating | honest band | follow the true level; note stale skills |
| Tell us about yourself | about 100 to 200 words | present work, one relevant past item, why this role, a forward-looking close |
| Situational or behavioral | compressed story, about 150 to 250 words | context, actions, result; first person, active voice; end on the result |
| Opinion or vision | one real stance, about 100 to 150 words | tied to experience, no hedge stack |

Length guide: single line gets one sentence; short answer two to four sentences; long box about 100 to 250 words. When unsure, go shorter. Respect any displayed limit.

Rules:
- Lead with the most relevant item; put one project per sentence for "describe your experience".
- If experience is adjacent, say so and pivot to what carries over. Do not bluff an exact match.
- Name the specific project, tool and team size instead of habits ("I always approach problems by ...").
- If a project has real users, say so once.
- Failure modes: copying the posting back; trait slogans; apologetic set-ups ("while I may not ..."); bare tool lists; padding with weak projects.
- **Legal, work-authorization, sponsorship, clearance and self-identification questions:** use only the candidate's own true answers. Never guess, never infer protected characteristics, never choose an answer to clear a validation gate. Voluntary self-identification fields default to the form's decline option only if the candidate has said so. Flag any agreement or consent box for the user to read.
- Keep a reusable answer file so wording stays consistent across applications. Review before each submit.

## 5. Cold and networking outreach

### 5.1 Pick the vehicle
- Formal application with a letter requested: cover letter.
- A named person, cold or warm, no open application: a short email (this section).
- Public professional updates: feed posts, not messages.
- Investor or partner outreach: not this skill's scope; keep the same honesty rules.

### 5.2 Facts first, angle second
1. Collect real, dated, public facts about the recipient or organization: a post, talk, paper, product change, job posting, public statement. Keep the source and date for each. Use an exact quote only with a link.
2. Only then write the angle, using only those facts.
3. If no real hook exists, say so and write a plainer message without fake personalization.
4. Leave out sensitive personal topics (health, money, politics, religion, family) even when public.
5. Removal test: if the personalized opening can be deleted and the message still makes sense, the personalization is decoration. The observation should lead into why the sender is writing.

### 5.3 Message shape (job-seeking email)
- **Subject:** short and human, naming the sender or the topic. No tricks, urgency, or role-title stuffing.
- **Hook (two to three sentences):** one specific, true observation about the company's product, method or claim, not "I love your mission".
- **Background (three to five sentences):** the current role in plain words, one relevant past item, one or two pieces of proof. Context beats a list of achievements.
- **Bridge (one or two sentences):** what the role or team seems to need and how recent work relates, stated as reasoned fit.
- **Disclosures:** state relocation, time zone, work authorization or an obvious experience gap early and calmly when material.
- **Link:** one portfolio or work link if useful.
- **Close:** one low-pressure ask plus name and email.
- Length: usually well under 300 words; shorter for a busy senior reader or a platform message.
- Do not paste a cover letter. Attach a résumé only when the market or the person's earlier reply makes that normal.

### 5.4 The ask
One ask per message. Make it easy to say yes or no, and smaller than a meeting where possible: two questions by reply, a pointer to the right person, a yes or no on whether the role is still open. Avoid vague asks ("pick your brain", "any advice") and calendar demands before any rapport. When the ask is information, say exactly what, and why this person.

### 5.5 Frameworks
Pick by recipient and purpose rather than filling a template:
- Observation, relevance, ask: for most job-seeking and expert outreach.
- Problem, evidence it matters, a credible approach: when the sender has a concrete contribution to offer.
- Situation, complication, question: for consultative contexts.
- Trigger, insight, curiosity ask: when a public event gives a real reason to write.

### 5.6 Style
- Write as a peer. Contractions are fine. Read it aloud and cut anything that sounds like marketing.
- More "you" than "I". Do not open with who you are.
- No "I hope this finds you well", no "just a quick question", no "excited to potentially join".
- Replace contrast reveals ("it's not about X, it's about Y"), triple lists of negations, and stacked em dashes.
- Do not claim familiarity. "Loved your talk" needs the talk's title. Never invent mutual contacts or prior conversations.

### 5.7 Follow-up
- Every follow-up adds something new (a relevant link, a small piece of work, an answer to a point in their post), never "just checking in" or "bumping this".
- A small number of touches, spaced out, each readable alone. A final short note that closes the loop is courteous; honor it.
- Stop at once on a no, an unsubscribe request, or any reply that ends the thread.
- Include an opt-out wording and the sender's real identity if sending to many people.
- Draft only. The user sends. Do not describe or schedule sends.

### 5.8 Platform messages
A connection note states one credible reason in a line or two (the platform limits note length; verify). After acceptance, one short message tied to the reason and a light question. Write it in the user's voice. Stop after a few unanswered messages.

### 5.9 Examples (invented placeholders)
- Informational ask: "Hi Dana, I read your team's post on moving support reporting to a shared warehouse. I run a similar migration for a 12-person finance team. Would you be open to answering two questions by email about how you handled backfills?"
- Referral ask: "Hi Sam, I am applying for the Data Analyst role at [company]. We worked together on the pricing review at [former employer]. Would you be comfortable referring me, or is there someone better to ask? I can send the posting and a two-line summary of the work."

## 6. Reference lists and reference requests

A reference list is a contact sheet, offered late in hiring, on its own page. It is different from a letter of recommendation, which is a written evaluation for an academic or fellowship file.

1. Choose people who can speak to the work: a recent direct supervisor, senior people who know the work, clients; for early careers, professors, advisers, internship supervisors, volunteer leads. Peers only when they observed the work directly and the context is industry. Follow a named institution's template where it differs.
2. Typically three to five names, when requested. Do not send with the first application unless asked. Ask permission each time before sharing.
3. **Permission request elements:** greeting; the role and organization; one line on what they know about the candidate's work; why their view matters; a clear yes or no ask that lets them decline; an offer to send the posting and résumé; thanks. Check they can give a strong, supportive reference in the relevant period and have current contact details.
4. **Briefing after yes:** subject with role and company; posting and résumé; two to four points the candidate is stressing; projects the reference may be asked about; expected timing; thanks.
5. **List format:** name, current title and organization, phone, professional email, relationship and timeframe. Header and font match the résumé.
6. Special cases: a current employer who does not know (offer prior colleagues or delayed contact); a manager who moved (list the new affiliation and the relationship); a weak relationship (do not list).
7. Integrity: never list someone who did not agree; never coach a reference to claim things that did not happen. Thank them afterward and share the outcome.

Academic recommenders need earlier notice and a packet (statement draft, résumé, program list, deadlines). Offer a short packet checklist; do not draft the recommendation itself as the candidate.

## 7. Offer replies and negotiation messages

Wording only. Do not generate market salary data; any figure comes from the candidate's own research.

1. First reply to a written offer: thanks, specific enthusiasm, ask when a decision is needed, and time to review if needed. If the offer is verbal, ask for it in writing.
2. Counter spine: thanks and enthusiasm; relevant true value; one specific ask; brief justification (research the candidate actually did, scope, a competing written offer only if real); openness to other elements (signing bonus, start date, review timing, title, development budget); a soft next step.
3. If base is fixed, shift the ask to elements the candidate really values.
4. When asked early for expectations, give a range the candidate owns, or defer to the role's budget.
5. Never invent competing offers, deadlines, or current pay. Where the law lets a candidate decline pay-history questions, redirect to the role. Legal rules differ by place; the candidate should check local rules.
6. No ultimatums. Ask that agreed terms appear in the revised written offer. Confirm final terms in writing; stay courteous if declining.
7. Some public-sector or union scales are fixed; the message then concerns step placement, start date or allowances.
8. Wait a day or two to prepare; do not accept on the spot.

## 8. Regional business English

Choose one locale per document: the reader's market, not the writer's by default. Keep one spelling system, one date order and one greeting ladder. If the reader mixes locales, use the receiving reader's.

| | US | UK | Australia | New Zealand |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spelling | color, organize, center | colour, organise (or -ize, kept consistent), centre | colour, organise, centre | colour, organise, centre |
| Date | January 15, 2026 | 15 January 2026 | 15 January 2026 | 15 January 2026 |
| Neutral greeting | Hi [Name] | Dear [Name] or Hi [Name] | Hi [Name] | Kia ora [Name] or Hi [Name] |
| Common sign-off | Best, Thanks, Best regards | Kind regards, Many thanks | Kind regards (new or formal), Cheers (once rapport exists) | Kind regards, Cheers, Ngā mihi (only if the organization uses Te Reo) |

Notes:
- UK formal letters: "Yours sincerely" with a name, "Yours faithfully" with "Dear Sir or Madam".
- Use Te Reo Māori only as complete phrases you understand and when the organization's own writing uses them. No decorative fragments.
- No forced local slang in professional writing.
- State currency and tax treatment in cross-border messages.
- Plain-word swaps that work in every market: "get in touch" for "reach out", "use" for "leverage", "follow up" for "circle back", "closer look" for "deep dive".
- Locale polish never licenses invented local presence ("our Auckland team") or pressure.
- For non-English drafts, locale tables here do not apply.
