Good writing starts before the first sentence. Decide who will read the piece and what that person should know, believe or do afterwards. A message written for everyone usually serves nobody, because the writer never has to choose what to leave out. Picture one real reader, think about what they already know and how much time they will give you, and let those answers shape the length, the vocabulary and the order of the material. If you cannot say in a sentence or two what the piece is for, keep thinking before you keep typing.

Most people write more clearly when they talk the idea through first. Explain your point aloud to a friend or a colleague, or imagine doing so, and notice where you stumble. The places where you hesitate are usually the places where the idea is still vague. Write down the plain version you just said, then improve it. A rough first draft that says something definite is easier to repair than a polished draft that avoids the point. Do not wait for the perfect opening line. Start in the middle if you must, and come back to the beginning once you know where you are going.

Put the most important thing early. Readers give the opening of a page more attention than any other part, and many will stop soon after. If the main point, the request or the conclusion sits in the last paragraph, some readers will never reach it. Say what matters first, then give the reasons and the detail in descending order of importance. This does not mean every piece must be blunt. It means the reader should never have to wonder why they are being given a paragraph. A short orientation at the top, followed by the substance, serves almost every kind of document.

Structure is a kindness to the reader. Break long material into sections with clear labels, and make each paragraph do one job. When a paragraph tries to handle two ideas, readers lose the thread of both. A good paragraph usually begins with a sentence that tells the reader what the paragraph is about, and the remaining sentences support or explain it. If you find a paragraph running past a dozen lines, look for the place where the subject changes and split it there. Headings should describe what follows rather than decorate the page.

Choose simple words when simple words are accurate. A short common word is rarely less precise than a long rare one, and it spares the reader a small amount of effort every time it appears. Use technical terms when your audience knows them or needs them, and explain them once when they do not. Avoid inventing new labels for ordinary things. If you introduce a term, use it the same way every time, because a reader who sees two different words will assume two different things are meant. Consistency in naming removes a surprising amount of confusion.

Sentences should carry one main thought. Long sentences are not wrong, but each extra clause is a promise the reader must remember until you pay it off. Read a long sentence aloud. If you run out of breath, or if the subject and the verb are so far apart that you forget the subject, divide it. Mix lengths on purpose: a short sentence after a long one gives the reader a place to rest. Prefer the active voice when the actor matters, and use the passive when the actor is unknown or unimportant. Neither is a sin, but a page of passive sentences feels distant and hides who did what.

Concrete beats abstract. A reader can picture a named person, a specific number or a particular afternoon, but they slide past phrases such as significant improvement or various stakeholders. When you notice an abstract noun, ask what it refers to and try to name the thing itself. An example often does more work than a paragraph of general explanation. Choose examples that your reader will recognise from their own experience, and keep them short enough that they illustrate the point rather than replace it. If you have no example, that is sometimes a sign you do not yet understand the point well enough.

Cut words that do no work. Many drafts contain openings that announce what the writer is about to say, hedges stacked on top of each other, and endings that repeat what was just said. Read each sentence and ask what the reader would lose if it were deleted. If the answer is nothing, delete it. Be careful, though, not to cut so hard that the prose turns into a list of fragments. Short does not mean clipped. The goal is that every word earns its place, and that the sentences that remain still sound like a person talking to another person.

Tone should match the occasion and the relationship. A note to a close colleague can be relaxed. A letter to a stranger who has authority over you should be courteous and direct. A report for a committee should sound measured and calm. Pay attention to how your reader will feel when they read your words, especially when the news is bad or the request is large. Be honest without being harsh, and be polite without hiding the point. When in doubt, imagine the reader sitting across the table and speak to them as you would in that situation.

Be careful with confidence. Say plainly what you know, and say plainly what you are unsure about. A writer who qualifies everything leaves the reader unable to tell which statements matter, while a writer who qualifies nothing loses trust the first time a statement turns out wrong. Place a doubt where it belongs, next to the specific statement it affects, instead of spreading general caution over the whole piece. If you need to give an opinion, label it as an opinion, and give your reasons so the reader can judge them.

Revision is where most of the quality comes from. Almost nobody writes a good draft on the first attempt, and the idea that skilled writers produce clean prose in one pass is a myth. Plan to read your work at least twice after you finish it. On the first pass look at the large shape: is the order right, is anything missing, does the opening fit the ending. On the second pass look at paragraphs and sentences. Leave the typing errors until last, because there is no point polishing a paragraph you may delete.

Distance helps. If you can, put the draft away for a few hours or overnight before you revise it. When you return, you will read it more like a stranger and notice gaps that were invisible while you were writing. When a deadline does not allow a night, change the way you see the text instead: print it, increase the font size, or read it aloud. Reading aloud is especially good at exposing clumsy rhythm, repeated words and sentences that sound nothing like speech. Mark every place where you stumble, and fix those places first.

Ask someone else to read your work when the stakes justify it. Choose a reader who resembles your intended audience, and tell them what you want to know: whether the main point is clear, where they got bored, where they got lost. Do not ask whether it is good, because most people will say yes to be kind. Watch where their attention goes if you can. When a reader misunderstands a passage, resist the urge to explain what you meant. The page has to work without you standing behind it, so change the page.

Feedback is information, not a verdict. When someone disagrees with a sentence, they may be wrong about the fix but right that something is off. Look for the problem under the suggestion. If three readers stumble at the same paragraph, rewrite that paragraph even if each of them proposes a different change. If one reader dislikes something that the others liked, consider it, but you do not need to act on every single comment. Keep a clear sense of what the piece is for, and use that purpose to decide which advice to take.

Formatting should help scanning without becoming the message. Lists suit items that are parallel and separate, such as steps in a process or a set of options. They suit reasoning badly, because the connections between ideas disappear when each one sits on its own line. Use bold type sparingly, since emphasis that appears everywhere stops meaning anything. Keep the same layout for the same kind of content throughout the piece. Leave white space between sections so the page can breathe. If you cannot tell whether a table or a paragraph would work better, try both and see which one reads faster.

Openings deserve special attention because they set the reader's expectations. Avoid beginning with a general statement about the importance of your topic, as in the claim that communication has always mattered. Readers have seen that opening many times and learn nothing from it. Start instead with the situation, the problem, the question or the result. Tell the reader why they should keep going in the most direct way you can. A good opening also tells them what kind of piece they are reading, so they know how to approach it and how long it will take.

Endings deserve the same care. A conclusion should leave the reader with something useful: the main point restated in a fresh way, a decision to make, the next step to take, or a question worth keeping. Do not end by apologising, and do not end by promising more. When the piece has made its point, stop. A short, calm last paragraph usually lands better than a long summary of everything that came before. If the document asks the reader to act, say clearly what the action is, who should take it and by when.

Titles and subject lines are read more often than the text below them. Make them specific enough that someone who sees only the title can guess the content. A title such as update tells the reader nothing, while a title that names the project and the news lets them decide whether to read on. For email, put the request or the decision in the subject line when you can. For longer documents, check that the headings, read in order without the body text, outline the argument. If they do not, the structure probably needs another look.

Transitions guide the reader from one idea to the next. They do not need to be elaborate; often a single word such as because, but or so is enough to show how two sentences relate. When two paragraphs seem disconnected, the problem is usually not the missing connecting phrase but a missing idea. Ask yourself what the reader should be thinking at the end of one paragraph and what question that thought raises. Begin the next paragraph by answering it. When the logic holds, the writing tends to hold together without much decoration.

Numbers need context. A figure alone is hard to interpret, so give the reader something to compare it to: last year, the usual range, the size of the group. Round numbers to the precision your reader needs, and no further. State units every time, and be consistent in how you write dates, currencies and percentages. When you present a table, say in the text what the reader should notice in it, because few people will search a grid of figures for the point on their own. Check every figure against its source before you send the piece.

Names, dates and quotations are easy to get wrong and expensive when you do. Check spellings of people and organisations, and confirm that titles are current. When you quote someone, copy the words exactly and make clear who said them. When you summarise what someone else wrote, make sure the summary would be recognised by the original author. If you are unsure about a detail, either find out or leave it out. A small mistake in a detail that the reader happens to know well can make them doubt everything else you say.

Write for readers who are busy and distracted. They are reading between other tasks, often on a small screen, and they may only skim. Short paragraphs, clear headings and a visible main point help, but so does a calm voice. Avoid forcing the reader to hold too many things in mind at once. If a procedure has steps, number them. If a decision has options, state the options and the recommendation separately. If a message needs a reply, say so near the top and say by when. The easier you make the next step, the more likely it is to happen.

Writing for an international audience calls for plain choices. Idioms, jokes and regional references can be baffling to people who learned the language as adults, and they are often lost in translation. Prefer literal words, keep sentences moderately short, and avoid stacking several nouns together to form a compound label. Spell out acronyms the first time you use them. These habits also help native speakers, who are just as likely to be tired or hurried when they read. Clear writing for one audience is almost always clearer for the others.

Habits matter more than inspiration. Writers who make progress tend to set aside regular time, even if it is short, and begin each session by rereading the last page. Keep a simple notes file for ideas, phrases and questions that come up during the day. When you get stuck, lower the standard for the moment: write a bad version of the next paragraph, or describe in plain words what you want it to say, and move on. You can improve the text later, but you cannot improve a blank page. Small, steady sessions beat rare heroic ones.

Learn from writing you admire. When a piece stays with you, go back to it and look at how it was built. Notice how long the paragraphs are, where it places its main point, how it handles examples and how it moves between ideas. Copy the structure, not the sentences, and see whether the same approach suits your material. Over time you will build a personal sense of what works. Reading widely, in many forms and on many subjects, trains your ear more reliably than any list of rules.

Finally, remember that rules are servants of the reader. Every piece of advice here, including this one, has exceptions. A very long sentence can be beautiful, a hedge can be exactly right, and a list can sometimes carry an argument. When a rule and the reader's needs conflict, serve the reader. Keep asking who is reading, what they need and what would make the page easier to follow. Answer those questions honestly, revise until the answers show on the page, and then stop.
